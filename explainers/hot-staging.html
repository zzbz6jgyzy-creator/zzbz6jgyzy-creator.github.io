Why Starship uses hot staging

Starship ignites its engines before separating from Super Heavy. The vented ring gives the upper stage’s exhaust a route out while the two vehicles are still together.

By Alex RReference review 23 Oct 2026

Explore diagramDetailed guideSources

Start with the five-stage cutaway, then follow the forces, exhaust flow and vehicle-generation changes below. The drawing represents the earlier vented-ring concept; the article distinguishes that hardware from the integrated V3 hot stage.

  1. 01 · The booster carries the stack

    Super Heavy accelerates the combined vehicle. Starship is still attached above the interstage. The enlarged view follows the joint between them.

  2. 02 · Prepare for upper-stage ignition

    The booster throttles down for staging. The ring between the stages is vented so that exhaust can escape when Starship’s engines ignite.

  3. 03 · Starship ignites

    The upper-stage engines light while the vehicles are still connected. Exhaust exits through the hot-staging ring. This is the moment the cutaway highlights.

  4. 04 · Separate under thrust

    Starship departs with its engines already running. The booster and ship now have separate jobs: continue the mission above, and prepare the booster’s return below.

  5. 05 · Continue on separate paths

    Starship continues accelerating. Super Heavy turns toward its return sequence. The hot-staging operation has ended; boostback is the next phase.

Why the sequence matters

A coast creates a pause between powered phases. Igniting before separation reduces that interruption, but introduces a demanding exhaust and separation problem. The ring is part of the hardware that makes that trade possible.

What the flight timeline tells us

SpaceX’s Flight 14 timeline lists booster MECO at T+2:20 and hot staging at T+2:22. Those are approximate event times for that flight, not universal timings for every Starship vehicle. [1]

1. What “hot” changes: event order

Documented mechanism

A conventional separation can shut down the first stage, release the connection, establish a gap and then ignite the next stage. NASA’s Crew-9 report describes that order for Falcon 9. NASA describes Starship’s upper-stage engines starting before the booster engines have completely cut off and before separation. The word hot describes the powered separation, rather than the atmospheric temperature or the temperature of the rocket’s outer skin. [7] [6]

Explanation

Three events that look simultaneous in a video remain distinct engineering tasks: starting engines, releasing the structural connection and establishing safe relative motion. Successful ignition alone does not prove separation succeeded. Equally, seeing a gap does not establish that the engines reached their intended operating condition. That is why a useful flight account separates propulsion performance from the separation outcome. This is a way to read the evidence, rather than a claim about SpaceX’s unpublished release logic.

2. The performance argument: time and mass

Physics

The ideal rocket equation relates available velocity change to effective exhaust velocity and the logarithm of the initial-to-final mass ratio: Δv = ve × ln(m0 / mf). It explains why carrying additional structure has a performance cost. It does not, by itself, calculate the payload benefit of changing a staging sequence; a real ascent also has gravity, drag, steering and mission constraints. [4]

Worked example

During an unpowered interval the rocket still moves, and may still gain altitude. It is not suspended in place. In a simplified vertical coast with constant local gravity and negligible drag, its upward speed changes by −g × Δt. Taking an explicitly assumed g = 9.8 m/s², a two-second interval changes upward speed by approximately −19.6 m/s. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured Starship loss or a prediction of payload gain. [4]

Explanation

Continuing thrust through staging addresses that interruption. It also changes the hardware the vehicle must carry and the loads the separation must withstand. The right comparison is a complete trajectory and mass budget. A claim such as “this adds a fixed number of tonnes” needs that supporting analysis, with the vehicle version and mission held constant; it cannot be read directly from the cutaway.

3. The hard part is the plume

Documented mechanism

NASA’s 2024 analysis describes upper-stage exhaust impinging on the booster shield inside a confined interstage. At the low ambient pressure of separation, the plume expands around the vehicle. The problem includes pressure loads as well as heating; a vent does not make those interactions disappear. NASA compared different geometries and engine-start sequences, including moving-body simulations, and reported favourable agreement with second-flight data. [6]

Explanation

Think of three questions when viewing the enlarged joint: where can gas leave, which surfaces can it strike, and how do the two vehicles move while that flow is changing? The red arrows show the intended exhaust route in the schematic. They are not a map of Mach number, heat flux or pressure. Turning an explanation into a credible prediction would require boundary conditions, material properties, mesh resolution and validation against measurements.

4. Thrust does not need something to push against

Physics

A rocket accelerates by expelling mass. The thrust equation includes exhaust momentum and the difference between nozzle-exit and surrounding pressure. Starship’s engines do not need to push on the booster to function. During hot staging, however, exhaust also strikes nearby structure, so the overall interaction contains more than the free-flight thrust of an isolated engine. [5]

Explanation

This distinction helps explain the drawing: the upper stage’s thrust accelerates that stage, while plume interaction imposes additional loads on the connected system. Once released, relative acceleration opens the gap. The visual model leaves out engine transients, structural vibration and propellant motion. Those omissions are useful for learning the sequence, but they are exactly the details a separation qualification programme has to investigate.

5. The hardware changed: earlier ring versus V3

Vehicle-generation fact

SpaceX’s V3 introduction describes an integrated hot stage replacing the previous single-use protective interstage. The forward dome of the booster fuel tank is exposed to the upper-stage exhaust, with internal tank pressure and a non-structural steel layer providing protection. This is a vehicle-generation change, not simply the old ring with a different label. [3]

Explanation

Read historical images with their flight and vehicle version attached. A detached interstage seen on an earlier flight does not establish that every later booster carries and discards the same part. The cutaway above teaches the earlier vented-ring concept. The table makes its scope explicit rather than silently presenting one geometry as the permanent design.

6. How to read the flight timeline

Flight-specific evidence

SpaceX’s Flight 14 timeline places most-engine cutoff at T+2:20, hot staging at T+2:22 and boostback start at T+2:27. The five-second difference between the last two listed events is arithmetic from approximate published times. It does not identify the exact instant a mechanical latch opened, or the duration of every engine transient. [1]

Explanation

A useful review records the vehicle generation, mission objective, engine-start outcome, separation outcome and booster-return outcome separately. A later failure should not erase an earlier demonstrated event; an earlier success should not certify every later event. When new footage arrives, compare like with like: the same camera angle, a reliable clock, and the mission’s own event descriptions. Keep launch schedules and future catch targets in the maintained tracker, where they can be updated without rewriting this engineering guide.

The next questions

Go a level deeper

Does cold staging mean the rocket stops moving?

No. It coasts. Its velocity changes under gravity and aerodynamic forces while propulsion is interrupted. The key difference is the order of separation and ignition.

Why is there no exact payload gain here?

A payload comparison needs a specified vehicle, mission trajectory, structural mass and propulsion assumptions. The ideal rocket equation alone cannot supply a defensible Starship-specific number.

Is the illustrated ring the current V3 configuration?

The main drawing represents the earlier vented-ring concept. The V3 comparison explains the integrated hot-stage change using SpaceX’s dated description.

A clearer picture

Common misunderstandings

The exhaust belongs to the upper stage

At the hot-staging joint, the key plume comes from Starship’s engines above the booster. The ring vents that exhaust outward.

The animation is not a flight simulation

The five stages explain the mechanism. Distances, plume shapes and playback speed are schematic; they do not predict a flight’s trajectory or payload gain.

Plain English

Key terms

Interstage
The structure connecting two rocket stages.
MECO
Engine-cutoff shorthand. SpaceX’s timeline expands it here as most engines cut off.
Ullage
The gas space in a propellant tank; a coast can also create a need to settle liquid before an engine start.
Plume impingement
Exhaust striking another surface, imposing thermal and pressure loads.
CFD
Computational fluid dynamics: numerical modelling of fluid flow.
Mass ratio
Initial mass divided by final mass for the burn being analysed.
