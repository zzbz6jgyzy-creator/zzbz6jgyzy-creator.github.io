Understanding Collision Evasion

Collision-assistance functions can warn or intervene in particular situations. Warning, emergency braking and engaging FSD are different behaviours; the name of one should not be used as evidence for another.

By Alex RReference review 23 Oct 2026

Explore diagramDetailed guideSources

Go beyond the animation: distinguish the functions, work through the physics of delay and braking, and read independent crash research with its scope intact. A release-specific Collision Evasion claim still needs the exact release notes for the vehicle, hardware and region.

Driver assistance requires an attentive driver. Consult the instructions for your vehicle and region.

  2. 2Developing hazard

  1. 01 · A hazard develops

    The overhead view shows a vehicle ahead in the path of travel. Positions are illustrative. No distance, speed or time-to-collision should be inferred from this drawing.

  2. 02 · Detection has limits

    Cameras and environmental conditions affect what a system can detect. The shaded area is a visual aid, not a specification of sensor coverage or guaranteed detection.

  3. 03 · A warning may be issued

    Tesla describes Forward Collision Warning as a visual and audible alert. That warning is distinct from a braking command. The driver remains responsible for responding. [1]

  4. 04 · Braking assistance may intervene

    Automatic Emergency Braking is a separate function that may reduce speed in certain collision situations. Its behaviour has conditions and limitations; the illustration does not show a guaranteed stop. [1]

  5. 05 · The driver remains responsible

    Do not rely on an assistance system to detect every hazard or prevent a crash. Use the instructions and limitations in your vehicle’s manual, including the requirements for any supervised driving feature. [2]

Why this diagram has no success score

A successful-looking animation cannot establish real-world avoidance performance. This sequence teaches the distinction between a hazard, a warning and a possible intervention. It is not an instruction to test the system.

1. Three different kinds of intervention

Documented distinction

A warning informs the driver. Braking assistance changes vehicle speed. Steering assistance changes the path. NHTSA distinguishes these functions even when several appear in the same car. A dashboard message, a brake-pedal movement and a steering input are therefore different evidence. Recording which occurred is more useful than calling every response “self-driving”. [2]

Vehicle-specific fact

Tesla’s Collision Avoidance Assist chapter separately describes Forward Collision Warning, Automatic Emergency Braking and Obstacle-Aware Acceleration. The last can reduce acceleration near a detected obstacle; it is not interchangeable with emergency braking. The same chapter’s approximate 5–200 km/h FCW/AEB operating range belongs to the specified US Model Y manual. It is not a guarantee for every model, direction of travel, software release or road condition. [1]

2. Closing speed is different from road speed

Worked example

Suppose two vehicles are travelling in the same direction. In a simplified constant-speed model, closing speed is the following vehicle’s speed minus the lead vehicle’s speed. If a follower is at 60 km/h and the vehicle ahead is at 40 km/h, the gap is shrinking at 20 km/h, or about 5.56 m/s. With a 20-metre gap, the constant-speed time to contact is about 3.6 seconds. These are hypothetical inputs, not a detection or activation threshold.

Explanation

Change the lead vehicle to a stationary object and the same follower closes at 16.67 m/s. At a 20-metre gap, the constant-speed time becomes about 1.2 seconds. Neither number predicts what a particular assistance system will do. Braking by either vehicle, a cut-in or a curved path changes the calculation. This is why a single speedometer reading cannot describe the complete collision problem.

Engineering model

Time to contact is a model, not a certainty. It depends on the assumption that relative motion continues as estimated. A real system must also decide whether an object is in the relevant path, whether its motion estimate is reliable and what intervention is appropriate. That conceptual chain explains why detecting something in an image and deciding to brake are separate tasks.

3. Delay and braking consume different distances

Physics context

Stopping distance includes travel before braking begins and travel while speed is being reduced. Rhode Island’s driver guidance separates perception, reaction and braking, and notes that traction changes braking distance. Assistance may change parts of the response, but it does not remove the physical distance required to slow the vehicle. [6]

Worked example

For an educational constant-deceleration model, distance = v × t + v²/(2a). Here v is initial speed in m/s, t an assumed delay in seconds and a a positive constant braking deceleration in m/s². The first term grows directly with speed; the second grows with its square. Doubling speed therefore doubles the delay distance and quadruples the braking distance when the assumptions stay the same.

Explanation

The chart assumes a one-second delay and 6 m/s² constant deceleration on a straight, level path. It omits tyre variation, grade, brake build-up, changing friction and a moving target. At 60 km/h, delay travel is 16.7 metres and modelled braking travel is 23.1 metres, totalling 39.8 metres. These are arithmetic results from chosen inputs, not measured Tesla stopping distances, safe following gaps or AEB performance specifications.

An assumption-led calculation

d = v × t + v²/(2a)

  • v = speed ÷ 3.6, converting km/h to m/s
  • t = 1 second, chosen delay
  • a = 6 m/s², chosen constant deceleration

Educational model only. A real collision-assistance system has different, situation-dependent timing and braking. Do not use the result to plan a following distance or test the car. [6]

4. What independent crash research actually found

Primary research

Jessica Cicchino’s 2017 IIHS study compared police-reported crashes in 22 US states during 2010–2014. It estimated rear-end striking-crash reductions of 27% for forward collision warning alone, 43% for low-speed AEB and 50% for warning with AEB. The corresponding injury-crash reductions were 20%, 45% and 56%. These describe the studied systems and populations, not Tesla’s current FSD or a Collision Evasion release. [4]

Explanation

Those percentages are relative reductions in crash-involvement rates, not a guarantee that an individual intervention succeeds. The population, exposure measure, comparison group and crash definition matter. A result for rear-end crashes should not be relabelled as a result for all crashes, pedestrian incidents or autonomous driving.

Primary research

A 2019 IIHS study examined the rear-end crashes that remained. Turning, lane-changing lead vehicles, unusual vehicle types, snowy/icy roads and higher-speed roads were among the circumstances overrepresented in crashes involving AEB-equipped striking vehicles. The finding motivates broader testing. It does not imply AEB causes every crash in those circumstances or gives an individual Tesla a numerical failure probability. [5]

5. Why false alerts and missed hazards both matter

Engineering model

A system must act on incomplete observations while the scene changes. Triggering too readily can create unwanted interventions; triggering too late can leave less distance to respond. This is a general detection-and-control trade-off, not a claim about Tesla’s internal confidence thresholds. A chart of visible camera coverage would not solve it: seeing an object does not establish its future path or whether an intervention is appropriate.

Explanation

The Model Y camera chapter identifies obstructions and visibility conditions as performance concerns. Clean lenses and calibration address prerequisites, not every ambiguity. Separate sensing from stopping physics: better braking cannot compensate for every missed observation. [7]

A useful incident record includes the vehicle, installed build, feature settings, conditions, displayed message and available unedited footage. Avoid guessing whether a visible slowing was a driver input or an automated action. Without the relevant evidence, an honest account can describe the observation while leaving the mechanism unresolved.

6. Where release-specific Collision Evasion fits

Explanation

The label Collision Evasion should be tied to the exact software release and its own description. It must not inherit every property of FCW, AEB or supervised driving merely because those functions share a vehicle. Likewise, the absence of that label in one public manual is not proof that no release contains additional behaviour. The correct question is which documented behaviour applies to which configuration.

Explanation

For an automatic-engagement claim, look for the release identifier, supported hardware, market, prerequisites, operating conditions and instructions for driver control. A social clip can be an observation; a complete primary release note is stronger evidence for the intended feature conditions. This review did not establish an accessible exact primary release note covering every re-engagement claim, so the guide does not turn secondary descriptions into operating instructions.

Explanation

Use the linked software tracker for current reports, then confirm the installed car’s own release notes and manual. A comprehensive explanation should make uncertainty specific: what is documented, what is calculated and what remains unverified. It should neither promise a rescue manoeuvre nor dismiss a reported feature simply because the name resembles an older safety function.

The next questions

Go a level deeper

Does the IIHS 50% result measure Tesla’s Collision Evasion?

No. It is a historical estimate for rear-end striking crashes among systems and vehicles in the 2017 study. It is not a Tesla-specific score or a current software evaluation.

Does an operating-speed range guarantee intervention?

No. A listed range is one operating condition. It does not guarantee detection, braking, avoidance or applicability to another model or market.

Why use a hypothetical stopping model?

It isolates how delay and speed affect distance. Its assumptions are shown so readers can understand the arithmetic without mistaking the result for a vehicle test.

A clearer picture

Common misunderstandings

A warning does not promise braking

Treat the alert and an automatic intervention as separate functions with their own conditions.

A demonstration does not establish availability

A clip from another car or market does not confirm a particular feature for your vehicle.

Plain English

Key terms

Forward Collision Warning
An alert about a possible collision risk; distinct from automatic braking.
Automatic Emergency Braking
A function that may apply braking in defined conditions to reduce impact severity.
Active safety
Assistance intended to help avoid or mitigate incidents while the driver remains responsible.
Closing speed
The rate at which the gap to another object decreases.
Time to contact
A prediction from assumed relative motion, not a guaranteed outcome.
Relative reduction
A change compared with a baseline rate; distinct from an individual success probability.
