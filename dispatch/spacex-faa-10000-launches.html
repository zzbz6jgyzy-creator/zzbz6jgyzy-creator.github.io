SpaceX · Launch policy · 28 Oct 2026

SpaceX tells the FAA it plans 10,000 launches a year. The airlines want launches at off-peak hours

SpaceX told federal regulators that it expects to conduct 10,000 launches a year on its own. That's about 55 times the 182 FAA-licensed US launches in all of 2025 (as of 28 Oct 2026). Its filing says the bottleneck is no longer rockets but the way the FAA closes airspace for them. Airlines for America, the airlines' trade group, filed in the same docket on the same day with a very different list.

What SpaceX filed

The letter is SpaceX's answer to a request for information from the Department of Transportation and the FAA, published in the Federal Register on 25 Aug 2026, about where to site new spaceports and how to give launches priority airspace in "critical space launch corridors." The notice asked for comments on or before 26 Oct 2026.

SpaceX's 24-page letter is dated 26 Oct, addressed to Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and signed by Mat Dunn, SpaceX's vice president of government affairs. It was posted to the public docket (FAA-2026-9736) on 27 Oct. You can read the full PDF.

The government's notice set the scale. It says the US was "responsible for 217 of the 329 launches worldwide" in 2025 and expects the rate "to reach 10,000 FAA-licensed launches and reentries a year by 2035." SpaceX's reply goes further: "SpaceX anticipates conducting 10,000 launches per year itself."

That number is a plan, not a schedule. The filing doesn't put a date on it, and because this was a request for information, the FAA hasn't proposed a rule or made any decision.

Musk's "just from Louisiana" line, checked

Musk posted at 2:07am ET on Wednesday, 28 Oct (11:07pm PT Tuesday; 06:07 UTC) that "10k Starship launches/year is just from Starbase Louisiana." The filing is more careful. It says SpaceX "is constructing a second facility in Starbase, Louisiana," and describes the planned spaceport as "designed for approximately 30 launches and reentries per day."

Thirty a day for a full year works out to about 10,950, but that figure counts launches and reentries together. For one site to reach 10,000 launches alone, almost every one of those operations would have to be a launch, every day of the year. The filing itself doesn't say how many of the 10,000 would fly from Louisiana.

The argument: rockets aren't the limit any more

SpaceX's main claim is that the FAA's air traffic control structure and processes are "outdated and incapable of supporting a materially higher launch or reentry cadence than current levels (182 licensed launches and 7 licensed reentries in 2025)." The filing credits FAA staff for supporting growth "from 32 licensed U.S. launches in 2020 to 182 in 2025," then lays out where the time goes today:

  • Airspace closures "still average just over two hours per launch," the filing says, citing the FAA's website, while the FAA's own concept for tailored corridors expects a normal vertical launch window of "less than 3 minutes."
  • Every launch repeats a planning cycle that starts about 10 days out. At 10,000 launches and reentries a year, SpaceX says, that cycle alone "would leave about 274 NOTAMs in coordination at any one time." NOTAMs are the official notices that warn pilots about hazards.
  • Decades-old lightning rules scrub or delay launches "that could otherwise fly safely," SpaceX says. By its internal analysis, modernizing them would increase launch availability "by up to 36% during peak LLCC violation seasons."

The filing also takes a swipe at other launch companies. In 2025, it says, SpaceX launched 101 of the 109 missions from the Cape Canaveral Spaceport "from only two pads," while all other operators combined flew 8 missions from 7 pads. If every operator matched SpaceX's rate per pad, the Cape alone "could host more than 450 launches a year." SpaceX argues: "The Government should not reward poor operator performance with additional land or subsidies."

What SpaceX is asking for

The core request is a set of charted launch and reentry corridors at busy spaceports, covering both launch and reentry. Inside a corridor, only an inner area around the rocket would be closed to aircraft. The filing calls this "a moving virtual bubble around the space vehicle." The outer part would stay open to aircraft on planned routes and be cleared only if something goes wrong. SpaceX's specific asks include:

  • For missions that fly inside a corridor, "airspace priority, automatic airspace approval within 48 hours of a request" to the FAA's Air Traffic Organization, "and a waiver of airspace fees."
  • "Multiple short windows per day, activated just in time and released automatically when the vehicle clears."
  • No environmental review for corridors. SpaceX writes that "NEPA review clearly does not apply."
  • Wider use of the FAA's Space Data Integrator, which feeds live rocket data to the FAA, and of SpaceX's own Cleared platform. The filing says SpaceX provides Cleared "at no cost to most major U.S. airlines."
  • For reentries, credit for an emergency text message, sent through the Wireless Emergency Alerts system, telling people under a failing vehicle's debris path to shelter in place.

SpaceX also puts a number on what an emergency reroute would cost an airliner. In a preliminary analysis of Cape Canaveral, it says, backup routes used only during emergencies "would add about 7 to 8 minutes to a Boeing 737-800 flying from LaGuardia to Miami."

The airlines filed the same day

Airlines for America, whose members include Alaska, American, Delta, JetBlue, Southwest, United, FedEx and UPS, submitted its own letter dated 26 Oct. It describes the FAA's forecast as an increase of "more than 460% to 10,000 launches per year by 2035" and says its members "anticipate serious impacts to their operations." Its five-page filing differs from SpaceX's on several points:

When to launchMultiple short windows a day, approved within 48 hoursSchedule launches "during periods of low volume use" of the airspace
Fees and incentivesWaive airspace fees for corridor missionsIncentives to minimize impacts on other users, through "commercial, financial, operational, and oversight criteria"

The two letters do overlap. Both want corridors that are active only for minutes before the airspace goes back to normal use, and both want debris maps computed in near real time if a vehicle breaks up. The disagreement is over who adjusts. SpaceX wants airlines to plan around charted corridors, and the airlines want launches timed around their traffic.

There's an odd wrinkle too. United and Southwest are both Starlink customers, fitting Starlink Wi-Fi across their fleets (see our stories on United and Southwest). Their trade group is now asking for launch windows timed around airline traffic, including for the rockets that carry those satellites.

What happens next

Comments were due by 26 Oct, and the docket showed 310 public comments as of 28 Oct 2026. Because the FAA called this a request for information, no rule has been proposed yet, and any corridor design would have to come later. SpaceX's filing shows the scale the government is planning for either way: it says the Space Force projects annual launches growing "from roughly 110 in 2025 to approximately 1,200 by 2036 at Cape Canaveral Space Force Station, and from 65 to approximately 1,800 at Vandenberg."

Sources: SpaceX comment, FAA-2026-9736-0263 (26 Oct 2026); Airlines for America comment, FAA-2026-9736-0236 (26 Oct 2026); DOT/FAA request for information, 91 Fed. Reg. 54909 (25 Aug 2026); docket FAA-2026-9736 comments on regulations.gov; Elon Musk on X (28 Oct 2026).
