Starlink answers Reliance: "ready to connect India." Here's who's really stuck in the queue, and why

After a week of Elon Musk calling Mukesh Ambani the "real boss of India," Reliance hit back overnight, saying it welcomes Starlink and that Jio's own satellite service is waiting too. On Friday Starlink's official account replied with something closer to a compliance report than a punch. Put the three licensed players side by side and the real bottleneck isn't one company. It's a security review with no deadline.

At 1:27pm ET on Friday, @Starlink quote-posted Reliance Industries' statement with a short reply: the company says it has "worked closely with regulators, industry and operators in India for 5+ years" and built "dedicated systems, gateways, and other infrastructure all in-country to exclusively serve Indian traffic," all "with the requisite government approvals." It closed with one line: "Starlink is ready to connect India with high-speed internet."

It's a noticeably cooler tone than the boss's. And it lands at the end of a week in which this turned into one of the loudest business fights on X.

How we got here

  • 7 Oct: Musk said Starlink's entry was being blocked by "certain oligarchs" protecting a "monopolistic chokehold," as quoted by The Hindu. The same day, SpaceX's Lauren Dreyer told India Mobile Congress, "We stand ready to serve India, as soon as India is ready!" per Moneycontrol.
  • 8 Oct: India's Ministry of Communications, per The Hindu, called the claim of unfair treatment "baseless and misconceived." Musk then posted that Starlink is licensed in over 165 countries and asked, "Is Ambani the real boss of India?"
  • 9 Oct: His "Dear Prime Minister Ambani" post went out. It has since passed 117 million views, by X's own count.
  • 14 Oct: Bharti chairman Sunil Mittal, whose group backs Eutelsat OneWeb, told the Bloomberg New Economy Forum he is "significantly ahead" of Musk in the queue because "we applied five years ago," per The Economic Times.
  • Overnight, 10:15pm ET: Reliance issued its first statement. "At Reliance, we have never feared competition," it said, adding that "Jio's own satellite services await clearances."
  • Friday, 1:27pm ET: Starlink's reply.

Three companies hold India's satellite licences. As of Friday, none of them can sell a single subscription.

The three licensees, side by side

Strip out the insults and the regulatory picture is surprisingly flat. India has granted satellite-internet (GMPCS) licences to three low-orbit operators, and the government says all three are "at broadly the same regulatory stage," per The Hindu:

  • Starlink (SpaceX): a telecom licence, then authorization from IN-SPACe, India's space regulator, in July 2025, per The Indian Express. Dreyer says it has built 20 gateway sites with hundreds of antennas, security controls tailored for India, and keeps Indian user data in the country, per Moneycontrol. Still waiting on security clearance and spectrum (The Hindu).
  • Eutelsat OneWeb (backed by Bharti Airtel): the first to apply. Mittal says its ground network has been ready for three years. Still waiting on "some final security clearances," in his words (ET).
  • Jio Satellite Communications (Reliance): licensed, and by Reliance's own account also awaiting clearances.

The order of the next steps comes straight from the government. Minister of State for Communications Pemmasani Chandra Sekhar said the telecom department has finished its part and the file now sits with the Ministry of Home Affairs for national security clearance, per Moneycontrol. After that comes spectrum. Communications Minister Jyotiraditya Scindia said spectrum will be assigned once the government settles a revenue-based pricing system, and that the telecom regulator TRAI and the telecom department are "close to final decision," per The Economic Times.

The awkward detail in Musk's framing

Musk's argument is that an incumbent is keeping Starlink out. But Jio isn't only a rival here. In March 2025, Jio signed a deal with SpaceX to sell Starlink equipment in India once the service is approved, as the BBC noted last week. Airtel signed a similar deal. So India's two biggest mobile carriers are, on paper, both Starlink competitors and future Starlink retailers.

That doesn't make the delay fine. It does make "one oligarch is blocking it" a hard case to prove when the oligarch's own satellite business is stuck in the same line. The government's stated worry, as The Indian Express reported, is control: foreign operators potentially bypassing Indian gateways. The BBC also points to smuggled Starlink terminals that Indian authorities recovered in 2024, in a drug case in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and during an army operation in Manipur.

Why Starlink's reply is the smarter move

Read Starlink's post again and notice what it leaves out. There's no mention of Ambani, no oligarchs, and nothing about monopolies. Every phrase answers the government's security concern instead: in-country gateways, traffic served "exclusively" inside India, "requisite government approvals." That speaks to the ministry that actually decides, not to Reliance.

My read: Musk's posts put a spotlight on a process that has no public deadline, and that pressure may well help. But the clearance that matters comes from India's Home Ministry, not from a billionaire's statement. Starlink's company account seems to know that.

What to watch next

  • The Home Ministry's clearance. It's the gate for all three. If one operator is cleared well before the others, the fairness debate starts again.
  • Spectrum pricing. Scindia's revenue-based charge decision is the second gate, and it sets what service in India will cost to run.
  • SpaceX's Q3 call on Tuesday, 20 Oct. SpaceX hosts an audio webcast at 4:30pm ET, per @SpaceX. India is the world's most populous country, and Starlink can't sell there yet, so listen for whether anyone asks about it.
  • Pricing promises. Musk wrote on 10 Oct that "SpaceX will need to make pricing affordable" in India. A launch price would be the first real test of that.
