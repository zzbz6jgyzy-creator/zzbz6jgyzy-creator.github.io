Who throttled Ukraine's Starshield? Three accounts of one speed cap, and what's actually confirmed

On Tuesday, minutes after SpaceX posted its Q3 results, The Wall Street Journal reported that Elon Musk ordered last week's speed cap on the military-grade Starlink service Ukraine uses, citing US officials and people familiar with the matter. That doesn't match what Musk wrote on X a week earlier, when he said limits on that service are "decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS." Here's each account side by side, the parts nobody disputes, and the deadlines that could bring real answers.

Updated 27 Oct 2026

What isn't in dispute

On October 14, Ukrainian officials reported that their access to Starshield had been restricted for devices moving faster than 100 mph. That's how four US senators summed it up in a letter to SpaceX on October 19, which says the restrictions came "without warning." Ukrainian officials said military-grade terminals stopped working when mounted on fast-moving objects, interfering with drones that rely on them for guidance, CBC News reported. The Wall Street Journal reported that the new speed limits disrupted drone attacks over Russian-occupied Ukraine, according to Ukrainian officials and military units.

Starshield is not the Starlink most people know. It's a SpaceX product for governments, with enhanced encryption and dedicated terminals, and it relies on the Starlink constellation as its backbone. CBC News reported that in Ukraine's case the Pentagon covers the fees, and the senators' letter says the service was "procured by the U.S. Government." That split, with a government customer on a network SpaceX runs, is why the question of who flipped the switch is hard to answer from the outside.

The service came back only partly. Mykhailo Fedorov, Ukraine's defence minister, posted on October 14 at 2:06pm ET (19:06 Dublin) that "Starlink connectivity at speeds of up to 160 km/h is being restored" and that "removing Starshield's speed limit entirely is now critical." On Monday, October 19, Sen. Richard Blumenthal posted that President Zelensky had told senators that "intelligence sharing & Starshield access are still partially degraded," The Hill reported.

Account one: Musk said the US government decides

Less than an hour after Fedorov's post, Musk replied on X at 3:00pm ET (20:00 Dublin) on October 14: "Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government. Any limits on speed or usage are decided by the US military and ultimately POTUS." In the same post he said commercial Starlink is limited to "civilian or defensive activities." The post has been viewed more than 4.5 million times.

Read carefully, it describes who has authority over Starshield. It doesn't directly say who made this particular decision.

Account two: a report pointed at the White House

Later that week, a Reuters report said the White House had ordered the restrictions, as CBC summarised it. CBC reported that President Trump denied he was involved in the decision, and said he didn't think Musk was involved either. Secretary of State Marco Rubio denied that the US caused the interruption, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned Zelensky to be "very careful about making statements like this that aren't true," CNBC reported.

On Monday, October 19, asked whether he had looked into who interrupted Starlink, Trump said: "I don't know who it was but I'd like to find out," according to the White House pool report.

Account three: the Journal says Musk ordered it

The Wall Street Journal's report, published Tuesday minutes after SpaceX released its earnings, says Musk "capped the speeds at which Ukrainian hardware could stay connected to the satellite-based network," citing US officials and people familiar with the matter. As CNBC relayed it, two people told the Journal that Musk and Trump discussed the move "soon after" it became public, and one of them said Musk raised it with several senior administration officials, including the president, before imposing the cap. The Journal also reported that Zelensky's complaints led to an informal investigation inside the White House.

This is reporting based on unnamed sources. The White House and an email associated with Musk didn't immediately respond to CNBC's request for comment, and CNBC noted the report wasn't mentioned on SpaceX's conference call with investors that evening.

These accounts don't fit neatly together. Who can actually set limits on Starshield, and who has to sign off, is a question about contracts and permissions, and it's what Congress is now asking for in writing.

What happens next, with dates

  • Requested, not yet done: In a letter announced on October 16, a bipartisan group of senators led by Thom Tillis (R-NC) and Jeanne Shaheen (D-NH) asked the Pentagon's Inspector General to investigate, including whether "actions or decisions by the Department of Defense or its contractors" played a part. They said they understand that at least some of the services are provided through contracts administered by the US Space Force.
  • Deadline: November 2, 2026. Senators Shaheen, Blumenthal, Sheldon Whitehouse and Chris Coons asked SpaceX's vice president of legal for answers and records on or before that date. Their letter raises questions about "the ability SpaceX has to restrict access to either Starshield or Starlink services."
  • Pledged: Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick (R-Pa.) vowed to hold any US officials who limited Starshield "fully accountable," per The Hill.
  • Reported, not confirmed: an informal White House inquiry, per the Journal.

Update, 23 Oct 2026: Trump said on Thursday that Musk “put it back,” but Ukraine’s drone pilots and President Zelenskyy say their tests still show the speed cap. What was claimed and what was tested →

My read

For SpaceX, this lands in an awkward week. The company just reported its Q3 numbers (our Q3 breakdown), and a large batch of locked-up shares starts unlocking on Thursday, October 22 (explained here). Starshield is a government business, and government customers want the service to do what the contract says, every time. Whoever made the call, the episode raises the question of whether one company's network can be throttled mid-war. It's a similar worry to the one behind Canada's push to diversify and Europe's telecom spectrum bid.

The November 2 letter is the date to watch. If SpaceX answers it, we could learn more about who can set limits on Starshield than any post on X has told us so far. I'll update this piece if Musk, SpaceX or the White House respond to the Journal's report.
