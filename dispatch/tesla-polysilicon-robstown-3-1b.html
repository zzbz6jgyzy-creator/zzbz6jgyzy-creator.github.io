Tesla wants a Texas tax break for a $3.1 billion polysilicon plant near Corpus Christi. Here's what its application says

From Alex R Tesla / Energy

Tesla has filed an application under Texas's JETI incentive program for a proposed polysilicon refinery near Robstown in Nueces County, the raw-material end of its plan to make solar panels in the US. The filing puts the investment at about $3.1 billion and promises 515 jobs by 2032. It is an application, not a decision. Tesla says the site is still "under consideration" and that without the tax limit it would not invest in Texas.

What Tesla filed

The Texas Comptroller posted application J0054 ("Tuloso-Midway ISD, Tesla Inc.") on 18 Nov. The application itself describes a "large scale, ultra-high purity polysilicon manufacturing facility" on parcels along State Highway 44 and FM 1694 near Robstown, west of Corpus Christi. Tesla calls it "the upstream component of Tesla's vertically integrated domestic solar manufacturing platform," making solar-grade polysilicon from ultra-pure trichlorosilane for N-type solar cells.

Polysilicon is the purified silicon that gets melted into ingots and sliced into wafers. Tesla's filing says "more than 90%" of the world's solar-grade supply is made outside the US, in China. The application doesn't give the plant's output in tons or how many gigawatts of panels it would feed.

The numbers in the filing

The economic study attached to the application was prepared by EY from data Tesla supplied. Its main figures:

ItemWhat the application says
Investment$3,108 million, 2027 to 2030: $1,500M engineering and construction, $1,425M equipment, $183M utility infrastructure
Spending by year$345M (2027), $1,019M (2028), $1,570M (2029), $174M (2030)
Jobs260 in 2030, 385 in 2031, 515 by 2032
PayAverage total compensation (wages and benefits) of $130,000; $67.0M payroll in 2032
Other operating spend$333M a year (utilities, vendors, maintenance) once fully running in 2032
Tax askSchool-district maintenance-and-operations value limited to $0 for 2028 to 2030, then 50% for 2031 to 2040

One inconsistency: the project description says the plant "would be constructed across two years," while EY's summary says "approximately four years." The spending table runs four calendar years, with 83% of the money in 2028 and 2029 (my math).

What the tax break is worth

JETI, the Texas Jobs, Energy, Technology and Innovation Act program, limits the value a school district can tax for maintenance and operations. EY's Table 12 lists the plant's taxable value before incentives each year, peaking at $1,641 million in 2031. Applying the requested limits to those values at Tuloso-Midway ISD's listed M&O rate of 0.7575 per $100 gives about $72 million in school M&O tax not collected from 2028 to 2040. That is my math from the filing's own tables. Neither Tesla nor EY states a total.

EY's tables also assume separate 50% abatements from Nueces County and Del Mar College for 2031 to 2040. Those would be different agreements, and this application doesn't show them as approved. With all of those incentives applied, EY's model has the plant paying about $247 million in local property taxes over 2028 to 2040 (my sum of its Table 13).

Two comparisons I find useful. The $3.1 billion works out to about $6 million of investment per permanent job. Nearly all of it goes into buildings and equipment, with payroll a small part of the running costs. And the roughly $72 million in school tax relief is small next to $3.1 billion, about 2.3%. That's the case Tesla makes: it says property tax "remains one of the most significant recurring costs" and that without the limit "the Applicant would not make the proposed investment in this state."

Where this sits in Tesla's solar push

Tesla has been building out US solar manufacturing all year. pv magazine reported in March that Tesla job listings describe a goal of 100 GW of US solar manufacturing before the end of 2028. That's a stated ambition. A refinery that starts spending in 2027 and hires its first workers in 2030 would supply that plan's later years, not its first ones. SpaceX is on a parallel track: its equipment deal with SINGULUS for a cell line in Bastrop, Texas, which we covered on 11 Nov, is a separate project at a separate company.

What happens next

The application asks for support from the Comptroller, the Governor's Office and the Tuloso-Midway ISD board, which all have a say under JETI. Tesla says it "continues to assess viable locations in Texas and other jurisdictions" and has filed a confidential supplement on the competing sites. It doesn't name them. Nothing in the filing commits Tesla to building, and no groundbreaking date is given.

My read

Polysilicon is the least glamorous part of a solar panel and, by Tesla's own account in this filing, the part the US makes least of, which is why this filing interests me more than another panel-assembly line would. The numbers are big for Nueces County: 515 jobs averaging $130,000 is roughly $67 million a year in payroll. They're modest for Tesla. What I'd watch is the missing capacity figure. Without tons per year, there's no way to tell whether this plant would cover a small share of Tesla's solar ambitions or most of it. Tell me below: should Texas give tax breaks like this to win plants, or should Tesla build where the economics work without them?

— Alex R
