SpaceX · Starlink · 25 Nov 2026

SpaceX turns down its BEAD money and offers to serve those homes for $0. What the government's new contract actually requires

The Commerce Department's broadband office published a model contract on Monday for a satellite provider that wants to serve its BEAD locations without taking the federal subsidy. NTIA's administrator says SpaceX has agreed to do exactly that. Households at those addresses would get Starlink equipment at no cost and the same speed and testing rules as any BEAD winner. In return, SpaceX skips most of the program's paperwork. Each state still has to sign its own copy, and none of that has happened yet as far as I can find.

What happened

BEAD is the $42.45 billion federal program for getting broadband to homes that don't have it. States run their own grant rounds, and NTIA, part of the Commerce Department, approves them. According to NTIA's announcement, it approved all 56 state and territory Final Proposals earlier this year. It also says some states "have been slow to sign contracts" with "large national LEO providers," and have imposed "a patchwork of obligations beyond BEAD programmatic requirements." The announcement doesn't name a company.

On Monday, 23 Nov, NTIA posted a model "Zero Outlay Agreement". It is for a low-Earth-orbit provider that will meet BEAD's service and testing rules at the locations it won "but that elects to decline BEAD subsidies." The first line of the contract reads "Amount of Federal Funds Obligated by This Agreement: $0.00."

The contract itself never names a company. NTIA Administrator Arielle Roth did, in a statement given to Broadband Breakfast: "SpaceX has agreed to abide by BEAD service and performance testing requirements at all locations awarded under a state or territory's Final Proposal at no cost to the BEAD program." Elon Musk's only comment was a one-word quote post, "Starlink," at 6:00pm ET (3:00pm PT) on Tuesday.

What households at those addresses get

I read all 15 pages. For each "Covered BSL" (broadband-serviceable location) listed in the contract, the provider has to deliver the following for ten years, counted from the day it certifies it can serve every address:

RequirementWhat the contract says
SpeedAt least 100 Mbps down and 20 Mbps up
Latency95% or more of measurements at or below 100 milliseconds
OutagesNo more than 48 hours a year on average, natural disasters excluded
EquipmentDish, router, power supply and cabling included at no extra cost, except standard shipping
Hookup timeService started within 10 business days of a request, with no network-extension charges
Low-cost planRequired, at least 100/20 Mbps; the provider may limit it to Lifeline-eligible households
DeadlineMust certify it can serve every covered address within four years of signing

The monthly price of service isn't waived. Customers still pay for a plan. The low-cost plan's price is a blank in the model (Exhibit B), so the provider fills it in, and the contract says the state "will not otherwise regulate" that rate. The equipment part is the big saving for households, since it removes the upfront hardware cost.

What SpaceX gets in return

This section gets less attention, and it probably explains why a company would pass on hundreds of millions of dollars. Because no federal money changes hands, the contract says the provider is not a "subgrantee" under the BEAD statute and not a "subrecipient" under the federal grant rules in 2 C.F.R. 200. In practice:

  • Audits and record requests are limited to checking the service obligations: test data, the semi-annual reports, equipment and low-cost plan records, and public notices.
  • No indemnity, insurance, letter of credit, bond or guarantee is required, "given that no Federal funds are provided."
  • If the provider misses an obligation, the state's only remedy is to take back the affected addresses and award them to someone else. The contract rules out damages, penalties, fines and attorney's fees.
  • The provider can't take any other federal broadband subsidy for those addresses during the term, including Universal Service Fund support, except money committed before the BEAD award.

States that don't want to use the model "will be responsible for identifying a provider to serve the applicable locations at a cost comparable to the cost approved under the state's original Final Proposal," NTIA says. That leaves a state with two choices: sign the $0 contract, or find someone else willing to do the job for about the same money.

How much money is involved

Roth's statement says the agreement has "the potential to save taxpayers hundreds of millions of dollars." Neither NTIA nor SpaceX has published an exact figure or a location count. Broadband Breakfast reported more than $700 million, citing a senior official, and the widely shared $738 million figure traces back to that briefing. Telecompetitor reported $636 million, citing administration officials. Until NTIA publishes a number, "hundreds of millions" is the only on-record figure.

My read

SpaceX is trading subsidy money for simpler contracts. My guess is that the extra conditions in state contracts were costing it more in time and legal work than the grants were worth, since its network already covers these addresses. For a family on a rural road, the deal looks like a free dish and a ten-year speed guarantee from a network that's already overhead. The part I'd watch is the low-cost plan, because its price is still blank. I'm also curious whether any state refuses and goes looking for a cheaper provider. Should states take the $0 deal, or hold out for fiber? Tell me below.

— Alex R
