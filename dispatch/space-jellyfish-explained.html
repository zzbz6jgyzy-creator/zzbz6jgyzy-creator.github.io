SpaceX · Explainer · 25 Nov 2026

A space jellyfish is a rocket plume lit by sunlight while the ground is still dark. The best window is 30 to 60 minutes before sunrise or after sunset

On a clear twilight launch, a Falcon 9 can leave a glowing, jellyfish-shaped cloud in the sky, visible hundreds of miles from the pad. Officials at Vandenberg Space Force Base, who also call the effect twilight phenomenon, say it usually appears two to three minutes after liftoff. I went through the official guidance, two NASA explanations, and two well-documented sightings. No twilight launch is confirmed in the next few days. SpaceX's upcoming USSF-380 flight from Vandenberg is a night launch.

What you're looking at

A space jellyfish is the glowing cloud you sometimes see after a rocket launch at dawn or dusk. The observer is standing in darkness, and the rocket has already climbed high enough that its exhaust is still in direct sunlight. NASA's Astronomy Picture of the Day used that name for a 4 March 2026 Falcon 9 launch from Cape Canaveral, which lifted off 52 minutes before sunrise. The second-stage plume was high enough to catch the rising Sun while the photographer was still in the dark.

The same lighting has a slightly older official name on the California coast. Vandenberg's fact sheet calls it twilight phenomenon: unburned propellant particles and water in the vapor trail freeze in the thin upper air and then reflect sunlight from high altitude.

How the cloud gets that shape

The exhaust itself is mostly water vapor and carbon dioxide, as NASA describes it. Once the rocket is high enough, that gas spreads quickly because the air around it is so thin, and the swollen, bell-shaped head of the cloud is that expansion. After the stages separate, the first-stage plume can hang as a broad body while the still-firing upper stage leaves a bright trail at the tip. A NASA Astronomy Picture of the Day from 17 February 2025, of a Vandenberg Starlink launch backlit by the setting Sun, shows the first-stage plume on one side and the upper stage at the apex.

Vandenberg says the frozen particles typically scatter sunlight into green, blue, white and rose, and that high-altitude wind can twist the cloud into a corkscrew. It usually becomes obvious two to three minutes after liftoff.

In October 2018, after a Falcon 9 evening launch from Vandenberg for Argentina's SAOCOM 1A satellite, a SpaceX spokesperson told Vice the company uses the term twilight phenomena, and that the effect is usually limited to launches within an hour of sunrise or sunset. That lines up with Vandenberg's 30 to 60 minute window.

Why Vandenberg evenings keep showing up in photos

Falcon 9s leaving Vandenberg typically fly south or southwest over the Pacific, on polar or high-inclination paths. If you are on the ground in Southern California after sunset, you can already be in darkness while the rocket, climbing out to the west, is still in sunlight. Jonathan McDowell, an astrophysicist at the Harvard-Smithsonian Center for Astrophysics, made that point to Vice after the SAOCOM 1A flight: going west from California, it can be dark for you, and the rocket can still see the sun.

That geometry is why West Coast evening launches show up so often in photographs from Los Angeles to Arizona. Space.com reported that a 28 September 2025 Vandenberg Starlink launch produced a jellyfish visible from Los Angeles to Phoenix. Florida produces them too, especially on pre-dawn flights, when the rocket climbs into the sunrise while the Space Coast is still dark.

The Iridium-4 night

One of the most widely reported examples on the West Coast came on 22 December 2017. SpaceX launched its Iridium-4 mission from Vandenberg at 5:27pm PT (8:27pm ET). Official U.S. Air Force photos from that evening also recorded a long, sunlit plume over the California twilight.

People across the West Coast reported a UFO. The Washington Post explained the next day that the light was the Falcon 9's sunlit exhaust. Kevin M. Gill photographed the plume from the Los Angeles area that night, with twilight still on the horizon and the Moon in the frame. That is the photograph at the top of this page.

A Florida dawn, October 2026

The East Coast had a clear recent example on 13 October 2026. SpaceX launched NASA's CRS-35 cargo mission to the International Space Station from pad 40 in Florida at 6:33am ET (3:33am PT), per SpaceX's own countdown post that morning. Chuck, who posts as @SpaceWithChuck, filmed the pre-dawn jellyfish in 4K from Central Florida and posted the clip the same morning. Other viewers posted video from Canton, Georgia, and from western South Carolina.

A later California example, from 22 April 2026, was photographed from the Del Mar waterfront by Karaj28 after a Vandenberg Falcon 9 Starlink launch. The bell of the plume is sharp against a dark blue sky, with the Pacific and a quiet street in the foreground. That is the shape people mean when they say space jellyfish.

How and when to catch one

Vandenberg says the phenomenon most often occurs with launches 30 to 60 minutes after sunset or before sunrise, as the booster rises out of darkness into sunlight. You need a clear sky. Cloud cover blocks the view.

Look in the direction of the launch, or along the rocket's path if you are far downrange. From Southern California, that often means looking west or southwest after a Vandenberg evening liftoff. From Florida and the Southeast, a pre-dawn Cape Canaveral launch can appear in the eastern sky and then arc out over the Atlantic. Give it two to three minutes after liftoff, which is when Vandenberg says the cloud usually becomes visible.

You can be a long way from the pad, as the Arizona, Georgia and South Carolina sightings show. Check SpaceX's mission pages for the exact liftoff time, convert it to your local time, and compare it with local sunrise or sunset. If the launch is 30 to 60 minutes on the dark side of either, and the forecast is clear, it is worth stepping outside. Our Can I see the SpaceX launch? page and the launch calendar are the place to start for pad direction and upcoming times.

What is not a twilight launch this week

I checked the next SpaceX flights as of 25 November 2026. USSF-380 is scheduled from Vandenberg on Saturday, 28 November, according to SpaceX's mission page. The official four-hour window opens at 11:39pm ET (8:39pm PT). Sunset at Vandenberg that day is 4:51pm PT, and astronomical twilight ends at 6:20pm PT, so the opening is almost four hours after sunset. That is a night launch, well outside the twilight window. SpaceX has listed a backup on Sunday, 29 November, at 9:26pm ET (6:26pm PT). That time is also outside the 30 to 60 minute window, so I am not treating it as a confirmed chance to see a jellyfish.

My read

A space jellyfish looks unnatural, which is why it keeps getting reported as something else. It is a lighting effect with a short, predictable window, and SpaceX now flies often enough that the window comes around. If one opens on an upcoming flight, I'll say so.

Would you get up before dawn for one, or do you wait for a sunset launch?

— Alex R
