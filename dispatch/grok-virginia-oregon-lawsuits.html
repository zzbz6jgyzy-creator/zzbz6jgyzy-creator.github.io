Virginia and Oregon sue SpaceX over Grok's sexualized images. Here's what the two complaints actually claim

From Alex R Grok

Two state attorneys general filed separate consumer-protection lawsuits against SpaceX, its AI unit SpaceXSI and X Corp. over images Grok generated of real people, including children, without their consent. Both suits say the companies told the public Grok was safe when it wasn't. These are allegations in a complaint. No court has ruled, and the companies have not filed a response yet.

The editor holds Tesla and SpaceX shares. Supercharged Daily is independent and not affiliated with Tesla, SpaceX or SpaceXSI. Nothing here is financial or legal advice.

Who sued whom, and where

Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones filed in the Circuit Court for the City of Richmond under the Virginia Consumer Protection Act, according to his office's news release dated Monday, 23 Nov. Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield filed in Multnomah County Circuit Court under Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act. The Oregon complaint is signed 22 Nov and was announced on 23 Nov in a release that calls it part of Rayfield's new "Keeping Kids Safe Initiative."

Both complaints name the same four defendants: SpaceX Super Intelligence Corp., SpaceX Super Intelligence LLC, X Corp. and Space Exploration Technologies Corp. That last one is SpaceX itself, which the Oregon filing describes as a Texas corporation. The two states filed separately but coordinated, and both complaints list the same outside law firm, Platkin LLP of Newark, New Jersey, alongside the state lawyers. Oregon also demands a jury trial and states that its claim exceeds $1 million.

What the states say happened

The core claim in both filings is deception. In the Virginia complaint's words, the companies "stripped out industry-standard safeguards and marketed a 'spicy mode' built to produce nudity," while assuring consumers that their products were safe. Rayfield put it more bluntly in his release: the lack of safety measures "was considered a feature, not a bug."

The headline number in both releases comes from the Center for Countering Digital Harm (CCDH), in a study published 22 Jan 2026. It estimated that between 29 Dec 2025 and 8 Jan 2026, Grok created and posted on X about three million sexualized images, including about 23,000 that appeared to depict children. The states turned that into a rate: roughly 190 sexualized images a minute, and one apparent image of a child about every 41 seconds. Those are an advocacy group's estimates, which the states cite. A court hasn't tested them.

The Oregon complaint also points to public statements it says were false. It quotes X's Safety account on 14 Jan 2026 saying it had "implemented technological measures to prevent the [@]Grok account on X globally from allowing the editing of images of real people in revealing clothing such as bikinis," and Elon Musk the same day: "I [am] not aware of any naked underage images generated by Grok. Literally zero." The complaint says researchers and reporters were still producing sexualized images after that date.

The timeline in the filings

29 Dec 2025 to 8 Jan 2026CCDH study window: about 3 million sexualized images, about 23,000 appearing to show children
On or about 8 Jan 2026Image generation on X limited to paying subscribers
14 Jan 2026X Safety announces new measures; Musk says "Literally zero"
Mar 2026Risk assessment updated to cover Grok Imagine, per the complaint
22 to 23 Nov 2026Oregon and Virginia file suit

One detail in the Oregon filing cuts both ways. It says reports from xAI and X Corp. to the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children rose from 116,850 in December 2025 to 220,234 in January 2026, and averaged 192,070 a month from January to June 2026, against 115,736 before Grok Imagine came to X. The state reads that as a measure of how much material Grok produced. The companies could just as easily point to the same figures as evidence that they were finding and reporting it.

What X says

X's Safety account posted a statement at 8:10am ET (5:10am PT) on Monday. It doesn't mention the lawsuits by name, but it answers them point by point:

The rest of the post says people who made this material "deliberately" broke X's terms and worked to get around its safeguards, that the company "acted promptly to tighten" Grok Imagine's protections earlier this year, and that classifiers now "screen every prompt before an image is generated and check every output after." It says accounts that try are actioned and reported to law enforcement.

What the states are asking for

Both ask the courts to stop the practices they describe, to make the companies give up money earned from them, and to award civil penalties and legal costs. Virginia also wants restitution for consumers. The caps are set by state law:

  • Virginia asks for up to $2,500 for each willful violation under Va. Code § 59.1-206(A), plus up to $1,000 per violation in investigation costs, "the exact number of violations to be proven at trial."
  • Oregon asks for penalties under ORS 646.642(3), which allows up to $25,000 per willful violation, in an amount "to be requested at trial." It also asks the court to treat every misrepresentation or omission as a separate violation.

Neither complaint puts a dollar total on it, and the number of violations is what decides the size. As a sense of scale only (my math, not a figure from either state): if a court counted each of the roughly 23,000 child images in the CCDH estimate as one Oregon violation at the maximum, that alone would be $575 million. Courts rarely land on the statutory maximum, and nothing says they will count violations that way.

My read

I own SpaceX shares, so I read these with that in mind. The legal theory is narrow on purpose. Both states built their cases around what the companies told consumers about safety, which keeps them on consumer-protection ground instead of trying to hold a platform liable for what users posted. That makes the 14 Jan statements the most important evidence in both files, more than the image counts. If SpaceXSI can show its safeguards did what X Safety said they did from that date, much of the deception claim weakens. If the researchers' later tests hold up, it gets harder. Every Grok Imagine change since January will now be read against these filings, and the defendants' first responses will show whether they plan to fight on the facts or on where the cases belong. Tell me below: should states be policing AI image tools through consumer-protection law, or is that a job for federal rules?

— Alex R
