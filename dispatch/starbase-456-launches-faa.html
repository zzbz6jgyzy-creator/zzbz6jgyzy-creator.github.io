SpaceX wants 456 Starship launches a year from Starbase

The FAA has opened a full environmental review of SpaceX’s plan to lift Starbase’s cap from 25 Starship launches a year to 456. Starship has flown three times in 2026. Here is what SpaceX is asking for, why it is asking now, how it compares with Elon Musk’s “~10k a year” goal, and the dates to watch.

From @ALJR86SpaceX desk · Analysis

On Wednesday, 14 October, the Federal Aviation Administration published a notice of intent in the Federal Register. It will prepare a Programmatic Environmental Impact Statement (PEIS) on SpaceX’s proposal to increase Starship–Super Heavy operations at its Boca Chica launch site at Starbase, Texas. The ceiling under study is up to 456 launches and 912 landings a year. SpaceX’s current authorisation is 25 launches and 50 landings.

That is about 18 times today’s cap. But the more useful story is in the details of the notice, and in how far the request runs ahead of the rocket.

What SpaceX is actually asking for

The FAA’s project page splits the plan into two phases:

  • Phase 1: up to 100 launches and 200 landings a year (100 Starship, 100 Super Heavy), on any day and at any time.
  • Phase 2: up to 456 launches and 912 landings a year, at any hour but only Monday to Friday. The FAA describes this as “a mature program launching more than 100 times annually” that “would be implemented within several years.”

Three things in the full notice stand out. First, the 912 figure caps landings at Starbase “regardless of the launch origin.” That leaves room for vehicles launched from other sites to come home to Texas. Second, night operations. The PEIS assumes half of all launches and landings happen between 10pm and 7am. Under the 2025 approval, only three of the 25 launches could be at night, and landings at the pad were daytime-only. Third, the FAA says no other site is a reasonable alternative, so the only option weighed against the plan is “No Action”: Starbase stays at the 2025 cadence.

At Phase 2’s full rate, five days a week, that works out to almost two launches every working day, plus about three and a half landings.

The cap is not the bottleneck yet

Starship has flown 14 times. Only three of those flights were in 2026: 22 May, 24 July and 28 September, as logged on our Starship flights tracker. There were five in 2025. Flight 14 was the first to reach orbit and deploy a real payload, 26 Starlink V3 satellites. SpaceX is nowhere near using its 25 slots.

So why ask for 456 now? Because the paperwork takes longer than the hardware. The FAA’s 2022 assessment allowed five Starbase launches a year. Raising that to 25 took until May 2025. A full environmental impact statement is a bigger job. Under federal law, the default deadline is two years from the notice of intent, which points to around October 2028. Agencies can extend it. Until the FAA signs a decision, Starbase stays at 25.

There is a legal angle too. Environmental groups sued over the 2022 approval, arguing the FAA should have done a full EIS. In September 2025, a federal judge ruled for the FAA and SpaceX. This time the FAA is doing the full EIS from the start. That route is slower, but likely harder to challenge in court.

456 is a ceiling, not a schedule. It is still the biggest Starship number any regulator has put on paper.

Next to Musk’s numbers

On 21 August, Elon Musk posted: “We’re aiming to reach 30+ Starship launches/day in 2030, which is ~10k annualized.” Starbase at full Phase 2 would deliver fewer than two a day.

Add Florida and the gap is still huge. The FAA’s February 2026 decision for Launch Complex 39A covers up to 44 Starship launches a year. For Space Launch Complex 37, the Air Force’s environmental study covers up to 76, and the FAA only opened its own review on 29 September. Starbase’s 456, plus 44, plus 76, adds up to 576 launches a year. That is under 6% of 10,000, and most of it is not approved yet.

That doesn’t make the 2030 goal impossible on paper. It does mean that roughly 10,000 launches a year would need many more pads and sites than anything filed today. Starship timelines have slipped before: our Promise Tracker logs the 2019 promise of orbit “in less than six months,” which came true in September 2026. The Starbase filing is SpaceX planning for success early. It is not proof the success is coming.

What to watch next

  • 10 November: the FAA posts its scoping presentation in English and Spanish. Requests for extra accommodations at the meetings are due by 2 November.
  • 17–19 November: public scoping meetings. In person on 17 November at Texas Southmost College, Brownsville, and on 19 November at the Port Isabel Event & Cultural Center (1–3pm and 5:30–7:30pm CT, 7–9pm and 11:30pm–1:30am Irish time). There is a virtual session on 18 November, 5:30–7:30pm CT (11:30pm–1:30am Irish time).
  • 30 November: scoping comments close (docket FAA-2026-12808). This is the window for locals to raise issues such as noise, night operations and beach access.
  • Flight 15: the working window is no earlier than 8 November on FAA planning slides, with the first serious attempt to catch a ship on the tower (see our ship catch piece). Hundreds of pad landings a year only make sense if ship catches become routine.
  • The draft PEIS: no date yet. That draft is where the FAA’s real noise, wildlife and airspace findings will show up, along with any mitigations SpaceX has to accept.

My read: this is SpaceX putting its long-term plan into federal paperwork. It is not a sign that Starship will fly 100 times next year. Starbase is capped at 25, and SpaceX has used three this year. The real news is that the FAA has started the clock on a review that could take two years. If ship catches start working and the flight rate climbs in 2027 and 2028, the 25-launch ceiling could start to bite before the review ends. The scale of the request also shows how far the company wants Starship to go.
