  3. Tesla’s December vote: three items, no shareholder proposals

Tesla · Shareholders · 19 Oct 2026

Tesla's December vote has three items and no shareholder proposals. Last year it had 14

Tesla filed its 2026 proxy statement on Monday, setting its annual meeting for December 3 at Gigafactory Texas. The ballot is short: re-elect Elon Musk, Robyn Denholm and JB Straubel, give a non-binding nod to 2025 executive pay, and ratify the auditor. The interesting parts are in the fine print, from a $158 billion pay figure that exists mostly on paper to a new way for retail investors to hand Tesla a standing vote.

What was filed, and when

Tesla's definitive proxy statement landed on the SEC's EDGAR system on Monday, 19 October (filing index). The meeting is on Thursday, December 3 at 4:00pm ET (1:00pm PT), which the filing gives as 3:00pm Central. That’s Thu 3 Dec, 4:00pm ET in your time zone. It will be streamed at virtualshareholdermeeting.com/TSLA2026 and webcast at tesla.com/2026shareholdermeeting. Only shareholders on record at the close of business on October 13 can vote, and online and phone voting closes at 11:59pm ET on December 2 (8:59pm PT), which is Wed 2 Dec, 11:59pm ET in your time zone.

In-person seats are limited, so Tesla says it will hold a random drawing. To enter, you have to have held shares on October 13 and register on Tesla's shareholder platform, according to the proxy.

At 10:31am ET (7:31am PT) on Monday, the company posted on X: "This year, we're asking you to vote 'FOR' all three proposals on the ballot."

The whole ballot, next to last year's

This is the clearest change. The 2025 proxy listed 14 items: six from Tesla, including the 2025 CEO Performance Award, and eight from shareholders, among them a proposal on whether the board should invest in xAI. The 2026 filing lists three, all from Tesla, and none from shareholders.

2025 meeting (Nov 6, 2025)2026 meeting (Dec 3, 2026)
Items on the ballot143
Tesla proposals63
Shareholder proposals80
Pay package voteYes, the 2025 CEO Performance AwardNo new award, only the advisory say-on-pay
Governance changeYes, removing supermajority rules (it failed)None

Sources: Tesla 2025 proxy and Tesla 2026 proxy. The comparison is my own.

The three 2026 items are:

  1. Re-elect three directors for three-year terms: Musk, board chair Denholm and co-founder Straubel. This is binding. Each needs a majority of the shares present in person or represented by proxy and entitled to vote.
  2. An advisory "say-on-pay" vote on 2025 executive compensation. It isn't binding. Last year's version passed with about 78.2% of the shares present or represented and entitled to vote, according to the filing.
  3. Ratify PricewaterhouseCoopers as auditor for 2026. This one is also advisory.

The proxy doesn't say why no shareholder proposals made the ballot. It does say the board has decided not to bring back its plan to scrap supermajority voting rules "at this time". That plan won support from about 39.4% of outstanding shares in 2025, short of what it needed to pass.

The $158 billion that mostly isn't there

SEC rules require Tesla to report Musk's 2025 pay at the accounting value his awards had on the day they were granted. That comes to $158,359,009,867. The median Tesla employee made $62,786, which gives a ratio of 2,522,203 to 1. That sounds like the biggest pay figure ever printed, and on paper it is. But two facts in the same filing change the picture:

  • About $26.1 billion of it is already gone. The "2025 CEO Interim Award", 96 million restricted shares approved in August 2025, was designed to be forfeited if the courts reinstated Musk's 2018 pay package. The Delaware Supreme Court did that on December 19, 2025. On April 21, 2026, the board's disinterested directors confirmed the forfeiture, according to the proxy.
  • None of the rest has been earned yet. The 2025 CEO Performance Award is about 423.7 million restricted shares in 12 tranches. Each tranche needs a market-cap milestone plus one operational milestone. "None of the tranches have become Earned Shares," the filing says.

Tesla also reports a "realized compensation" figure, meaning what Musk actually took home. On that basis, it says, his ratio to the median employee for 2025 was "0.00:1". The filing adds that Musk "received no compensation in 2025" beyond those two awards. So the say-on-pay vote is mostly a vote on how the pay plan is built, not on money already paid.

How much of Tesla does Musk control?

The ownership table puts Musk at 699,580,882 shares, or 19.8%, as of September 30. That count includes 286.4 million restricted shares from exercising the 2018 award, which carry votes. Of his shares, 207.5 million are pledged as collateral for personal loans.

The 19.8% is worth a second look. Tesla says it based its percentages on 3,952,648,559 shares outstanding, but it made "corresponding adjustments" for Musk. By my math, his 699.6 million shares are 17.7% of the full share count, and they come out to 19.8% only if you leave out the 423.7 million unearned award shares. Those unearned shares can still vote, just not the way Musk chooses: an irrevocable proxy casts them in the same proportions as every other share. So in December, they will count, but they won't tilt the result either way.

New: a standing vote for retail investors

Denholm's letter says retail investors "face technological barriers to traditional voting". Tesla's answer is a Voluntary Retail Voting Program, which the proxy describes as already adopted and backed by a no-action letter from SEC staff. After you vote this year on platforms such as ProxyVote.com or Say by Robinhood, you can sign up to give Tesla a standing instruction for future meetings, not this one. There are two choices:

  • Vote with the board's recommendation on everything, or
  • Vote with the board on everything except contested director elections and any acquisition, merger or divestiture that needs shareholder approval.

You can override the standing instruction by voting yourself, or cancel it at any time for free. Tesla says it will remind you at least once a year. With retail investors holding such a big share of Tesla, this could matter a great deal for votes that are close. And the merger carve-out is the one to watch.

No merger item, and the deals already on the books

Last week, Barclays said a Tesla–SpaceX merger was becoming "market consensus" and could be announced before this meeting. Nothing in the proxy proposes one. Neither company has confirmed any talks, and a deal would need its own filing and vote. What the related-party section does show is how closely tied the companies already are:

  • On January 16, 2026, Tesla agreed to invest about $2.0 billion in xAI Holdings Series E preferred stock. On February 2, 2026, SpaceX bought xAI Holdings, which owns xAI and X.
  • In 2025, Tesla recognized about $430.1 million in revenue from xAI, "primarily for the sale of our Megapack products". It recognized another $78.1 million through February 2026.
  • In 2025, Tesla also booked about $143.3 million in revenue from SpaceX, mostly vehicle sales, and paid SpaceX about $11.4 million under commercial and licensing agreements.

What the letter claims, and what it doesn't

Denholm's letter is a highlight reel. Some of it describes things that have happened: Cybercab production at Gigafactory Texas, Robotaxi in more US cities, more than 10 million vehicles delivered, the Semi launch and the Next Gen Roadster reveal. Other parts are forecasts, like Model Y being "on track" to be the world's best-selling vehicle this year, or plans such as the Terafab chip initiative and the first Optimus production lines. The letter gives no new numbers. Those should come on Wednesday, when Tesla's Q3 update streams live on X at 4:30pm CT, which is 5:30pm ET (2:30pm PT), or Wed 5:30pm ET in your time zone.

My read

After 2025's 14-item ballot and its fight over a new CEO pay plan, this is a ballot designed to pass without drama. The real news is about future votes. A standing "vote with the board" option, with a merger exception built in, shows Tesla planning for meetings that will be much less routine than this one. If a Tesla–SpaceX deal ever reaches shareholders, how many retail investors picked the second option could matter as much as any analyst note.

The ballot comparison and the 17.7% share calculation are my own. The proposals are what Tesla is asking shareholders to approve; none have been voted on yet.
