BEHIND THE VIEW

A world worth looking closer at.

Light that follows the present.

“Right now” calculates the Sun’s position from the current UTC time. Day and night follow geography as you move the camera. The orbiting camera is for exploration; it is not Earth’s real rotation speed. Day study and Night study are artistic lighting presets. Local civil time is shown for featured places; other coordinates show UTC. The sunlight calculation is approximate and is not for navigation.

Satellite infrared, with an actual frame time.

The cloud view uses EUMETSAT’s global infrared composite. The published frame time is read from the source metadata and pinned to the requested image. The product advances in three-hour steps and may be delayed. A composite combines observations from different satellites and is not an instantaneous photograph. Contains modified EUMETSAT data.

Cold, bright infrared regions are used as a visual cloud proxy. This does not detect every low cloud, and cold surfaces can resemble clouds; it is not a complete weather or cloud-classification product. Polar coverage is masked beyond 67.5° north and south. Missing coverage does not mean clear skies. Infrared brightness is converted to opacity for the globe. A source request can be slow; illustrative clouds remain identified while it loads. If the feed fails, the previous dated frame or illustrative fallback is labelled. Frames older than 12 hours are marked older; frames older than three days or dated in the future are rejected.

Why not closer?

The camera stops about 2,500 km above the surface. The global imagery used here is historical and is not sharper than that yet, so a closer view would only be a blur. There is deliberately no street-level view; the limit will be lowered if better imagery is added.

Lighting studies
Light

From home to our galaxy.

The Moon and planet positions are calculated for the current UTC time using Astronomy Engine, in the J2000 reference frame. Distances and physical body radii use kilometres, astronomical units and light-years. Labels mark worlds too small to resolve at the current scale; their markers are enlarged for visibility. Orbits show calculated paths around the Sun.

Nearby stars use selected HYG v4.1 catalogue positions and proper motions, originally at epoch 2000. Their distances have measurement uncertainty, and the catalogue is incomplete. The outer Milky Way is an illustrative structural model: a barred spiral with a disk roughly 100,000 light-years across and the Sun about 26,700 light-years from its centre. The orientation uses the IAU galactic coordinate system. Its synthetic stellar population and schematic arms are not a measured map of every star, a live photograph or a spacecraft-navigation tool.

The sky panorama is a 2009 photograph by ESO/S. Brunier, resized, converted and projected on a sphere. Deep sky changes the viewing exposure; it is a composite with the Earth render rather than a single camera exposure. The panorama fades away as the viewpoint leaves the Solar System. Other worlds use historical planetary maps; source notes identify enhanced colours, filled gaps and polar processing.

Detail that adapts.

Auto balances sharpness, data use and performance. High requests 4K surface detail; Ultra requests 8K surface imagery; satellite infrared is available at up to 4K. Studio requests a 16K NASA Earth surface and 8K night lights on sufficiently capable desktop devices; its large image is downloaded only when selected. Terrain normals and a separate ocean mask add surface detail and reflections. The image is historical, not a current global photograph. GPU limits can reduce resolution. Pause the globe whenever you like. Reduced-motion preferences are respected.
