Tesla Optimus: Built in Fremont.
Still learning the job.

01 / Perception

A world to understand.

A humanoid must estimate its surroundings and its own body at the same time.

How it works

Cameras provide information about objects, surfaces and motion. Joint-position sensing supplies proprioception: an estimate of where the limbs are. Contact and force feedback add information that an image can miss, such as whether a grasp is slipping.

Why it’s difficult

Recognizing a cup is only a first step. The robot also needs an actionable estimate of where it is, how it can be reached and what may change during contact. Occlusion, reflections, lighting and moving people complicate that estimate.

Tesla’s public record

Tesla’s stated engineering scope includes perception and physical interaction. Its published material does not provide a complete, final production sensor bill of materials here.

This overview explains the roles of sensing. The diagram marks a topic, not the verified location of a particular production camera.

02 / Manipulation

The hardest part is contact.

Dexterity is an interaction between mechanics, sensing and control.

How it works

Tendons can transmit actuator force through a compact hand. Finger geometry and compliant surfaces shape the contact; tactile sensing can help detect pressure or slip. A successful grasp requires enough force to hold an object without crushing or damaging it.

Why it’s difficult

A rigid box, soft fabric and a slippery cylinder demand different contact strategies. Tendon wear, routing friction, protection, repair and repeatable assembly all matter when a hand has to work repeatedly rather than perform one showcase grasp.

Tesla’s public record

Tesla’s hand manufacturing role specifically describes tendons, tactile sensors and soft goods. Those are development areas, not a complete production hand specification.

A degree of freedom is an independently describable motion. It is not a success-rate measure, and finger joint count need not equal actuator count.

03 / Actuation

Turning energy into movement.

Motors and transmissions must produce useful force without making the robot too heavy or inefficient.

How it works

An electric motor produces rotation. A transmission can trade speed for output torque; a screw mechanism can translate rotation into linear motion. Feedback from sensors allows a controller to regulate joint behavior rather than merely send an open-loop command.

Why it’s difficult

More gearing can increase available torque while affecting speed, friction and the inertia felt at the joint. Heat, mechanical play, packaging, maintenance and how a joint responds to an unexpected contact are part of the same engineering trade-off.

Tesla’s public record

Tesla has shown successive hardware revisions. The illustration is a generic motor-and-transmission explanation, not a cutaway of a proprietary Tesla actuator.

An attractive motion clip does not disclose the torque-speed curve, continuous thermal limit or service life of a joint.

04 / Whole-body motion

Every step is a control problem.

Walking and carrying cannot be treated as entirely separate skills.

How it works

The feet exchange forces with the floor. Moving a leg changes the available contacts; reaching or carrying changes the motion and loading of the whole body. A controller must coordinate those changes while respecting reach, friction and actuator limits.

Why it’s difficult

A smooth floor, a slippery patch and a stair are different contact problems. Quasi-static balance can be explained with a support region, but dynamic walking also depends on momentum and timing. A simple center-of-mass sketch is not a complete walking controller.

Tesla’s public record

Tesla’s overview identifies balance and navigation as necessary software capabilities. Public walking sequences demonstrate motion in their shown conditions; they do not establish universal terrain handling.

The principle diagram explains contact and balance. It is not a Tesla simulation or a measured stability envelope.

05 / Physical intelligence

From examples to useful behavior.

Training a behavior and deciding how it is controlled during a demo are different questions.

How it works

Demonstrations can provide examples of how to act. Training adjusts a policy from data; at deployment, a controller uses observations to choose actions. Runtime teleoperation instead places a human in the control loop. A system can also combine autonomous behavior with occasional assistance.

Why it’s difficult

Good performance on familiar examples is not enough. A robot needs to handle variation, recover when something goes wrong and operate within a defined task and safety envelope. The evaluation must say what help, resets or prior setup were required.

Tesla’s public record

Tesla describes end-to-end learned sorting in its 2023 film, and its data-collection roles describe human motion-capture work. Neither statement establishes that every public demo is autonomous.

This loop is an explanatory model. It does not claim Tesla uses another company’s published VLA architecture or disclose a proprietary network size.

06 / Systems engineering

A useful shift, not just a good clip.

Energy, heat, wear and repairability determine how long a robot can do useful work.

How it works

A battery supplies motors, computing and auxiliaries. Repeated motion creates electrical and mechanical losses that appear as heat. Load, speed, pauses and the task sequence affect average power; charging time and service interruptions affect operational availability.

Why it’s difficult

A factory needs predictable output and recovery procedures, not simply a headline battery figure. Hands, joints, covers and sensors must survive repeated contact. Safe shutdown, fault detection, training for operators and maintenance access are part of deployment.

Tesla’s public record

The Q3 call identifies durability and continued hardware refinement as open work. Tesla’s 2.3 kWh disclosure was about the 2022 prototype; it is not used here to predict a current robot’s runtime.

No independently verified full-shift uptime, final production battery capacity or current certified runtime is established by this guide.

01 / Approach

See it. Reach it.

Estimate the object’s position and choose a grasp that avoids collisions. A changed camera view or an occluded edge can change the plan.

02 / Contact

Feel the difference.

Grip force must hold the object without crushing it. Slip, compliance and unexpected contact make feedback essential.

03 / Recovery

Make failure recoverable.

A useful robot needs to notice a missed grasp, adjust and try again safely. A perfect selected clip does not reveal how often recovery is needed.

Held. Enough force to lift, not enough to crack.

The gap between a demo and a working day

One success is a moment. Reliability is a system.

Long tasks chain many small actions. Even a high success rate at each step can compound into a much lower chance of a flawless sequence.

Illustration: 0.95²⁰ = 35.8%. Assumes independent steps with identical success probability.

The questions that matter next.

How long can it work? How often does a person intervene? What happens after an error? Can the same system adapt to a changed task?

Real failures can be correlated, and recovery can improve completion rates. This simple model is a way to understand the problem, not a forecast of Optimus performance.

09 / The useful answers

Everything you wanted to ask.

Clear answers, with sources and the limits of what is publicly established.

What is Optimus?

Tesla’s humanoid robot. Tesla’s stated goal is a general-purpose machine for work that is repetitive, dangerous or unwanted. It is now in early production in Fremont, California, but Tesla has not shown it doing a full working day for a customer.

Tesla: AI & Robotics → · Tesla Q3 2026 update →

Is Optimus in production?

Tesla says yes: its Q3 2026 update reports that production began on its high-volume Fremont line and calls the output “the first production-quality bots” it has made. It has not said how many. In July, Tesla said early builds would go to its Optimus Academy to collect training data.

Tesla Q3 2026 update → · Tesla Q2 2026 update →

When can I buy one?

Not yet. On the Q3 2026 call, Musk said he expects deliveries to companies outside Tesla around the end of 2027, with industrial users first. Home robots come later as production grows. These are targets, not dates.

Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript → · Not a Tesla App: Optimus production begins →

How much will Optimus cost?

Tesla has not published a price. Musk has given aspirations: under $20,000 at high volume (2022) and $20,000 to $30,000 at scale (2024). In October 2026, Tesla’s finance chief said cost was trending toward where Tesla expects Cybercab costs to land, adding it was too early to say it was there.

Engadget: AI Day 2022 → · Digital Trends: We, Robot → · Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript →

What is Gen 3?

Tesla’s January 2026 update called Gen 3 “our first design meant for mass production”, with a new hand. Not a Tesla App reports that the Fremont line builds that design; Tesla has not formally unveiled it. Renders leaked from Tesla’s app in September 2026 and are unconfirmed.

Tesla Q4 2025 update → · Not a Tesla App: Optimus production begins → · Not a Tesla App: Gen 3 app leak →

How dexterous are the hands?

Tesla described 11 degrees of freedom per hand in 2022 and for Gen 2 in 2023, with touch sensing on every finger. The production Gen 3 hand is reported at 22. Musk said in October 2026 that the hand is still being refined, and Tesla engineer Konstantinos Laskaris called it “one of the most challenging hardware areas”.

Not a Tesla App: AI Day 2022 hardware → · Not a Tesla App: Gen 2 improvements → · Not a Tesla App: Optimus production begins → · Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript →

How tall and heavy is it?

The only published height is from the 2021 concept: 5 ft 8 in (173 cm) and 125 lb (57 kg). Gen 2 was said to be 10 kg lighter than its predecessor. Tesla has not published Gen 3 dimensions.

TechCrunch: the Tesla Bot is coming → · Not a Tesla App: Gen 2 improvements →

Is it autonomous or remotely controlled?

It depends on the demonstration. Tesla described sorting and cell handling as autonomous, and Musk said the 2025 kung-fu clip was AI, not teleoperation. Musk said the 2024 shirt folding was not autonomous, and Bloomberg reported human help for some We, Robot interactions. The archive below labels each case.

Tesla: Sort & Stretch → · Tesla: battery-cell handling → · Interesting Engineering: Optimus kung fu → · Musk’s shirt-folding clarification → · Bloomberg report via Business Standard →

How does Optimus learn?

Ashok Elluswamy, who leads Tesla’s AI software, said in October 2026 that Optimus has to learn by watching people do a task and then practising it, because, unlike the car fleet, there is no exoskeleton for people to get into and puppet the robot. Tesla also hires operators to record motion-capture data.

Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript → · Tesla: data collection role →

Does it run the car’s FSD software?

It shares Tesla’s vision and computer work, but a humanoid also has to control balance, contact and dozens of joints. Musk says the next computer, AI5, is needed because Optimus has far more degrees of freedom than a car, and expects it in Optimus around April 2027.

Not a Tesla App: AI Day 2022 hardware → · Q3 2026 earnings-call transcript →

Who runs the program?

Ashok Elluswamy, who also leads Tesla’s Autopilot software, took responsibility for Optimus when Milan Kovac left in June 2025.

Bloomberg via Gulf News: Optimus leader leaves →

How does it compare with other humanoids?

Others are further along in some ways. Agility’s Digit works at named customers, 1X takes deposits for a home robot, Unitree lists a robot from $13.5K, and Boston Dynamics showed a production Atlas bound for Hyundai. Tesla’s distinctive bet is scale: a line designed for a million robots a year.

Agility Robotics: Digit → · 1X: NEO → · Unitree: G1 → · Engadget: Atlas at CES 2026 → · Tesla Q1 2026 update →

Why a humanoid rather than a robot arm?

A human-shaped robot can in principle use spaces and tools built for people. That flexibility costs more joints, balance and contact problems. A fixed arm is often better for one well-defined station.
