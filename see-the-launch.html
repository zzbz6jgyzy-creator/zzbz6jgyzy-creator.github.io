    Permission, calibration & accuracy

    On iPhone or iPad, tap Start and allow Motion & Orientation access. On Android, allow the browser’s motion sensors. If an in-app browser blocks access, open this page directly in Safari or Chrome.

    Hold the screen nearly flat, face up. The top edge shows where you are pointing, in portrait or landscape. Move away from magnets, metal cases, vehicles and speakers; gently move the device in a figure eight if the reading is unstable.

    Magnetic readings are corrected locally using your selected observing spot and the World Magnetic Model 2025. This is an approximate heading cue, not precision navigation or live rocket tracking. The model covers 2025–2029; unsupported readings stay clearly labelled.

    Some tablets and computers have no usable compass sensor. The north-up pad bearing remains available. JavaScript is required for live sensors; the static bearing works without it.

    03 / READ THE SKY

    A launch is more than a bright dot.

    THE TWILIGHT EFFECT

    Dark ground.
    Sunlit exhaust.

    A high plume can catch sunlight while you stand in twilight. That contrast can create the “jellyfish” appearance. The geometry makes it possible; it does not guarantee a bright display.

    NASA explains the twilight effect
    01 / THE FIRST MINUTES

    Start with the pad bearing.

    Look toward the pad for liftoff and the initial climb. The ascent may then shift across the sky. Our separate scenario direction is illustrative, not a tracking instruction.

    02 / AN OPEN HORIZON

    Low angles need space.

    Nearby buildings, trees, terrain and haze can hide a rocket even with favourable geometry. Adjust your horizon angle to explore that limitation.

    03 / YOUR EYES, YOUR CAMERA

    Photos can look brighter.

    Long exposures and phone night modes can reveal faint exhaust your eyes barely detect. A spectacular image elsewhere does not mean the same view from your location.

    04 / NOT EVERY LIGHT IS A LAUNCH

    Know what you are watching.

    A Starlink train, a satellite pass and a launch plume are different sights. Use Identify a Starlink for satellites after deployment.

    Your before-you-go checklist

    1. Recheck the source. Launch targets can slip; a countdown is not confirmation of liftoff.
    2. Choose a legal public spot. Respect closures, private land and local instructions. Check Kennedy visitor information, Vandenberg notices or Cameron County closures.
    3. Check your weather. Clouds, haze and storms can change quickly. Ground-level forecasts do not measure the complete line of sight.
    4. Give yourself time. Set up before the published target and keep the official broadcast handy for holds or changes.

    04 / UNDER THE HOOD, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

    A useful model.
    A clear boundary.

    01

    A real pad and your position

    Pad coordinates and your selected location give a great-circle distance and initial true bearing. An unrecognised pad stays unknown rather than defaulting to Florida.

    02

    A hypothetical ascent point

    We place a point at a chosen altitude and downrange distance along a typical heading. A spherical Earth calculation gives its elevation and horizon footprint. This is not an actual trajectory, launch-window analysis or visibility guarantee.

    03

    Sunlight at the right place and time

    Approximate solar position uses the launch instant and observing coordinates, independent of your device’s time zone. Day-only targets have no daylight estimate. Sunlit exhaust geometry does not predict plume brightness.

    04

    Forecast intervals that match

    NWS cloud data is shown only when a valid forecast interval contains the launch target. Missing coverage is unknown. Cloud cover alone is neither a launch-weather clearance nor a scrub prediction.

    Sources, updates and privacy

    Launches use the site’s cached Calendar source, normally refreshed hourly when the job succeeds. Editorial targets need manual review. No visitor calls Launch Library directly. City search uses a local subset of GeoNames (CC BY 4.0); maps use public-domain Natural Earth. The map projection uses MIT-licensed D3 helpers.

    Your location and preferences stay in this browser. Device location and compass access require an explicit tap. NWS receives coordinates only after a forecast check. Shared links exclude location unless you choose to include a rounded location.

    Solar geometry reference: NOAA solar calculation notes. Forecast source: NWS public API. Neither agency endorses this tool.

    Not modelled: terrain, atmospheric refraction, actual doglegs, vehicle attitude, stage burns, brightness, local obstructions beyond your chosen angle, cloud along the entire path, or rocket noise. No travel or viewing outcome is guaranteed.
