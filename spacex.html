02 / The fleet explorer

Different machines.
One bigger ambition.

Launch vehicles, spacecraft and a constellation do different jobs. Explore the architecture and what each programme makes possible.

Launch vehicle

Falcon 9

The orbital workhorse.

Falcon 9 is a two-stage launcher for satellites, Dragon and other payloads. Its architecture separates the job of lifting away from Earth from the upper stage’s job of delivering a mission to its selected orbit.

The first stage has nine Merlin engines. The second stage has a vacuum-optimised Merlin. SpaceX lists a reference height of 70 metres; published payload maxima should not be mistaken for what every recoverable flight can carry.

The useful distinction is between the primary mission and recovery. Payload deployment, first-stage landing and a later reflight are separate events. The launch board gives you the next schedule entries; the engineering sequence explains the two paths.

Heavy-lift launch vehicle

Falcon Heavy

Three cores. A different mission envelope.

Falcon Heavy combines three Falcon-derived cores in its first stage. Their 27 Merlin engines provide the initial lift, with an upper stage handling the onward mission.

Its configuration addresses missions that need more performance than the single-core vehicle. Recovery plans depend on mission energy and hardware choices; a photo of two side boosters returning is not proof that all three cores were recovered.

Use the mission’s own payload, orbit and recovery plan when comparing flights. A maximum payload table describes an envelope with assumptions, not the payload of the next launch.

Crew & cargo spacecraft

Dragon

A spacecraft with people at the centre.

Dragon is a spacecraft, not the rocket below it. Falcon 9 supplies the launch, while Dragon performs the rendezvous, on-orbit and return portions appropriate to its mission.

NASA describes crew capacity of up to four on its missions, within a design capable of more passengers. Crew and cargo variants have different jobs. The capsule and trunk are distinct parts; returning the capsule does not mean the entire spacecraft assembly is reused.

For a crew flight, launch is the start of a larger sequence: spacecraft activation, rendezvous, docking, time on station, undocking and recovery. The schedule can change independently at each stage.

Development programme

Starship

Build. Fly. Learn. Repeat.

Starship pairs the spacecraft and upper stage with the Super Heavy booster. The programme is designed around recovering and reusing the whole system, rather than only a first stage.

The development record should be read flight by flight. A booster catch, a ship reentry, an orbital flight and a later reflight are different achievements. Hardware revisions also change the vehicle, so a single old specification should not become an eternal description of the programme.

Our flight lab follows objectives and observed outcomes alongside future targets. It is the place to compare missions and inspect the evidence without turning a planning date into a guaranteed launch.

Connectivity constellation

Starlink

An orbital network, not one satellite.

Starlink combines spacecraft, ground infrastructure and user equipment into a communications network. The satellite fleet contains different hardware generations, so one generic spacecraft drawing cannot describe every vehicle in orbit.

Launch totals, satellites in orbit, working spacecraft and orbital-shell placement answer different questions. None alone proves service quality at an address. Local approvals, equipment and network conditions still matter.

Our observatory keeps count categories separate and gives orbital source dates. Its sky finder offers geometric candidates, not guaranteed naked-eye sightings or direct operator telemetry.

03 / Follow the mechanism

One launch.
Two journeys.

Explore a conceptual Falcon mission. The upper stage carries the mission onward; a recoverable booster follows its own return path.

ILLUSTRATED PRINCIPLE / NOT A GROUND TRACK / NOT TELEMETRY

01 / Leave the pad

Two stages. One climb.

A Falcon 9 mission begins with the first stage providing the initial climb. Its nine Merlin engines burn RP-1 and liquid oxygen. The payload and second stage ride above it.

This is a conceptual sequence, not a mission clock. Timing, trajectory and recovery plans vary.

SpaceX · Falcon 9

02 / Separate

The mission splits into two journeys.

After first-stage engine shutdown, the stages separate. The second stage continues accelerating the payload; the booster follows its own flight plan.

Stage separation is not payload deployment. The upper stage still has work to do.

SpaceX · Falcon 9

03 / Continue to orbit

Altitude is only part of the job.

The upper stage adds the velocity needed for the selected mission. Orbit insertion and payload deployment depend on the destination and mission plan.

Reaching a high altitude alone does not establish orbit. The path here is an illustration, not a calculated trajectory.

SpaceX · Falcon 9

04 / Recover the booster

A separate path back.

On recovery missions, the booster manages its return and landing while the upper stage continues its task. Recovery may use a droneship or a land-based site; some missions expend the stage.

Booster recovery and payload success are separate outcomes. Do not assume every mission attempts a landing.

SpaceX · Mission milestones

05 / Inspect and refly

Landing makes reuse possible.

Recovery is followed by inspection, preparation and a later flight. A successful landing is a milestone in that process, rather than proof that the hardware is immediately ready to launch again.

Falcon first-stage reuse is distinct from Starship’s goal of recovering and reusing both stages.

Selected milestones

How extraordinary
became repeatable.

A few turning points in the story. These are historical milestones, rather than a live count of launches or recoveries.

  1. 2008

    A privately developed rocket reaches orbit

    Falcon 1 reaches Earth orbit, establishing a foundation for the larger launch systems that follow.

    SpaceX · Mission milestones
  2. 2012

    Dragon reaches the space station

    The first Dragon visit to the ISS extends commercial cargo access to the orbiting laboratory.

    SpaceX · Mission milestones
  3. 2015

    The first Falcon land landing

    The Orbcomm mission brings a Falcon 9 first stage back to Landing Zone 1 on 21 December.

    SpaceX · Mission milestones
  4. 2017

    An orbital-class booster flies again

    The 30 March Falcon 9 mission demonstrates first-stage reflight, followed by another recovery.

    SpaceX · Mission milestones
  5. 2020

    Crew Dragon carries astronauts

    Demo-2 launches on 30 May with Bob Behnken and Doug Hurley, beginning the crewed Dragon flight era.

    NASA · Demo-2 launch
  6. 2024

    Super Heavy meets the tower

    On 13 October, Flight 5 achieves the first tower catch of a Super Heavy booster.
