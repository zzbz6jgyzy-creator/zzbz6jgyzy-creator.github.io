The Supercharged Daily review

Model SThe electric grand-tourer.

Drivers researching a low-slung flagship, a large liftback and long-distance comfort.

Editorial buying analysis6 min guideReviewed 25 Oct 2026
The verdictDrive & characterCabinChargingSoftwareOwnershipYour checklist

The recommendation

Why this Tesla?

The flagship that made the long-distance electric sedan desirable remains a compelling used-market study. Its low shape, large liftback and performance character deserve a proper assessment of generation and condition, not a comparison based only on the price of a new Model 3.

01 / Drive & character

A flagship with a purpose beyond its badge.

Model S made a persuasive case for an electric car as an aspirational touring machine: a low body, substantial cabin and the possibility of strong performance without a conventional drivetrain. Today its appeal is specific. It is for someone who wants that flagship shape and character, with the purchase assessed as an individual car rather than a generic trim name.

Tesla’s current US product page says Model S is no longer in production and directs buyers toward available inventory. This review therefore approaches it primarily as used-car research. It does not imply a new factory order is available, or that one remaining example represents every car sold during the model’s long history.

The differences between earlier cars and the refreshed dual-motor/Plaid generation matter to the driving experience and ownership. Suspension, steering controls, infotainment, charging and driver-assistance hardware should be identified before comparing prices. A lower used price can open the flagship door; understanding what is behind that door is how to make the most of it.

02 / Cabin & daily use

A liftback, not a conventional sedan boot.

Model S has a large rear hatch. That is an important correction to a common shorthand about its sedan silhouette: the opening is part of its practical appeal. Test long bags and awkward loads in the actual car, with the seats arranged as you would use them. Do not transfer a cargo figure from a different model year without checking how it was measured.

The cabin and controls changed substantially across generations. Later cars use a landscape central screen and a driver display, but equipment and rear entertainment differ with year. Steering-wheel or yoke fitment should be confirmed on the actual example. The right question is whether you enjoy the fitted controls on a normal drive, including parking and low-speed manoeuvres.

Seat condition, climate performance, glass, trim and powered functions should all be part of the inspection. A flagship’s appeal is most convincing when the whole cabin works as intended. Take a proper seat in the rear, try the driving position for more than a few minutes and check that the storage spaces solve your own touring routine.

Check the charging source

Tesla’s North American Supercharging support lists up to 250 kW for the Model S family at V4 sites, but that is not a promise for every historic Model S. Older batteries, charging hardware and vehicle condition can produce a very different result. Identify the exact generation before comparing a used car’s charging capability.

For a touring car, charging and efficiency should be read together. The same stop adds a different amount of onward distance depending on speed, conditions and consumption. A large battery can store more energy without accepting peak charging power throughout the session. Neither capacity nor one peak number establishes a 10–80% time.

Confirm AC capability, connector compatibility and any regional retrofit or adapter requirements on the specific car. Historic Model S specifications can differ from current-car assumptions. For a used purchase, a practical charging demonstration and documented equipment can be more valuable than borrowing a number from a later brochure.

04 / software

Buy the hardware and entitlements that are present.

The connected Tesla experience is a major part of the attraction, but older Model S vehicles span very different hardware eras. Processor, camera suite, connectivity and driver-assistance equipment cannot be inferred from the exterior. Read the vehicle’s information screens and establish what is installed, what is paid for and what remains supported for that car.

Do not assume a seller’s reference to free Supercharging, an old FSD purchase or a connectivity package transfers without conditions. Ask for the relevant terms and confirm the benefit through the appropriate Tesla process. An entitlement that was once active and a benefit that will belong to the next owner are different things.

Where FSD (Supervised) is available, attentive human supervision is still required. A later vehicle’s demonstration is not evidence of an earlier car’s feature set. Our recommendation is to value the screen, controls, navigation and assistance you can verify today; treat future capability as an open question rather than a line item on the purchase invoice.

05 / ownership

A flagship inspection is an investment in the experience.

Identify year, battery, drive unit, suspension and wheel specification before looking at headline range. Review the service record and obtain realistic insurance and tyre quotes. Test air-suspension operation where fitted, listen on different road surfaces and check doors, windows, climate and charging. A specialist inspection can help turn a promising advert into a well-understood purchase.

Battery condition and warranty are individual-car questions. Do not treat the displayed full-charge range as a laboratory capacity test: calibration, configuration and recent usage can affect what you see. Ask for documented diagnostic information when available and understand what it does and does not measure. Remaining warranty depends on the specific vehicle and applicable terms.

Model S makes most sense when you actively want its combination of low shape, liftback practicality and flagship character. Compare the right generation against alternatives, then buy on condition and documented equipment. Its significance in electric-car history is real; the best ownership experience still begins with the actual example in front of you.

The questions worth asking

A little more clarity.

Specific answers for the car you are researching.

Can I order a new factory-built Model S?

Tesla’s current US page states that production has ended. Research actual inventory or used examples and check availability in your market.

Does Model S have a hatch?

Yes. It is a liftback, despite its sedan-like profile. Try the opening and rear-seat arrangement with the loads you carry.

Does every Model S charge at 250 kW?

No. The family-level support figure is not a historic-car guarantee. Battery generation, fitted equipment and conditions must be checked.
